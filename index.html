The Striper Recount

In August 2026, NOAA corrected its estimates of recreational fishing trips, and the striper numbers came down with them. Anglers caught far fewer fish than the old estimates said. That doesn’t leave more fish in the water: stock assessments work backward from the catch, so if the fishing rate holds, a smaller catch points to a smaller population. Watch the song, then work the numbers yourself.

The short version: four answers for your fishing buddies

  1. How much did the recount change?

    NOAA found its mail survey counted far too many fishing trips, striper trips included. Across the whole record the corrected count has about 58 striper trips for every 100 in the old one. The other 42 never happened, which is the song’s “down about 42 percent”.

  2. Fewer fish caught. So more fish left?

    No. Stock assessments work out how many stripers there are from how many were taken out. If the fishing rate holds, a smaller catch means a smaller population, not more fish left.

  3. Did anglers’ share of the fish killed change?

    Yes. It shrank with the survey fix, and it will shrink again if the assessment adopts a new study showing about half as many released stripers die.

  4. So what happens to the rules?

    The benchmark assessment will work out what the corrected count means for the stock, and the Board sets the rules from it. Nobody knows the outcome yet. To follow along, see “What you can do” on the site.

What you can do

  • Follow the process. The Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission posts the striped bass assessment, Board meetings and public comment periods on its striped bass page. When the Board considers new rules, anyone can send a comment.
  • Follow, subscribe, and donate to ASGA, the American Saltwater Guides Association.
  • Log your catches in GotOne to improve recreational angling data. GotOne data goes to scientists, researchers, and state agencies. The shared data is anonymized, so you don’t blow your spots. GotOne co-produced this site.
